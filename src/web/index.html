    Where these numbers come from

    Everything on this page comes out of 1.07 million real sales at a British gift and homeware supplier: every order it took between December 2009 and December 2011 (the Online Retail II records).

    We line each product up week by week, what it sold for and how many went out, then compare every product only against its own past. That last part matters. Without it you would just be measuring the obvious, that cheap things sell in bigger numbers than dear ones, which tells you nothing at all about your own price.

    Three things it can't tell you

    • It's what tends to happen, not what will. Nobody ran an experiment here. Prices moved for reasons: sales, seasons, clearing out old stock. Those same reasons moved the sales figures too. So read this as evidence worth having, not a forecast you can bank.
    • Takings are not profit. A discount can bring more money through the till and still leave you worse off at the end of the week. Put what a unit costs you in the box on the left and you'll see both numbers side by side.
    • One shop, one country. This is British gift and homeware, priced in pounds. Which way a price change pushes usually travels to other trades. The exact size of it doesn't. That's what the twelve other trades above are for.

    Where the other trades come from

    Reading the small print is the part that can't be automated. Three of those files had a price column, a sales column and a perfectly believable shop story behind them. We dropped all three because their own paperwork says the numbers were invented: a made-up car seat dataset, a made-up grocery discount trial, and a textbook example where the prices and sales were generated rather than recorded. They would have fitted the maths beautifully and meant absolutely nothing.
